Inside the published book

What could make a moral reason binding?

Metaethics asks what moral claims mean, what could make them true, and why they would give anyone a reason to act.

In the fictional city of Port Meridian, a commission sets out to construct a moral system everyone has reason to obey. Its first task proves more demanding than drafting rules: before proposing a whole system, the members must explain what could establish even one objective moral reason. The continuing story gives that abstract problem a human setting, while keeping the invented cases separate from evidence about the real world.

Three proposals receive sustained attention: grounding reasons in human welfare, deriving requirements from our capacity to choose and act, and treating some reasons as basic. Advocates develop their arguments, critics press objections, and replies lead to revisions. Throughout, the inquiry distinguishes having a reason from feeling motivated, moral truth from knowledge of that truth, and an obligation from permission to enforce it.

A fourth approach asks whether ordinary facts about suffering, relationships, and human commitments already explain moral criticism and correction. This is the book’s case for moral anti-realism: evaluation need not depend on a further moral foundation that exists independently of every standpoint. Stilwell favors that account while leaving it open to stronger arguments. Difficulties with the proposals examined are not a proof that every possible form of moral realism is false, and someone’s indifference does not erase the suffering at stake or other people’s reasons to respond.

Written for curious readers, students, and discussion groups, the book combines a continuing narrative with explicit philosophical argument. Diagrams, a glossary, argument reconstructions, and an index help readers revisit the central distinctions. It can be read independently; the companion volume How to Test a “Moral” System applies related questions to complete ethical frameworks within the Valuemetrics collection.

One project, two forms

The labs isolate the questions. The book makes the whole journey.

Each interactive on this site holds one problem still: meaning, authority, reasons, evidence, standing, value conflict, disagreement, responsibility, or what remains after moral realism. The published book develops the foundational dispute through connected arguments and dialogue, so that a distinction practiced in a lab can be examined within a sustained philosophical exchange.

Earlier site edition · seven-part outline

A construction project, not a verdict announced in advance.

Examples, imagined cases, and written exercises make the reader explain each step. Every part records what survived, what was given up, and what still lacks support.

  1. I

    Specify the thing you hope to build

    Fix the meanings of objective, moral, true, binding, knowable, coherent, and action-guiding before testing begins.

  2. II

    Give the system content

    Name who counts, what receives a verdict, what matters, how values generate rules, and how conflicts are settled.

  3. III

    Find the source of the “ought”

    Separate motivation, commitment, agreement, law, pressure, and practical support from authority over an informed dissenter.

  4. IV

    Ask how the system could be known

    Test intuition, genealogy, disagreement, evidence, factual inputs, uncertainty, and correction without borrowing the conclusion.

  5. V

    Let the great candidates make their best case

    Give consequence, duty, virtue, care, sacred order, fair construction, local standards, pluralism, and hybrids their strongest hearing.

  6. VI

    Inspect the last repairs

    Audit reform, public codes, power, dissent, responsibility, moral emotion, and every remaining route to the promised authority.

  7. VII

    Continue after the search

    Preserve facts, relationships, emotions, criticism, and politics, then begin public reasoning from named human stakes and revisable commitments.

The result the journey earns

No candidate returns with the full specification intact.

Each approach examined in the book faces at least one problem. It may depend on what a person or divine being values, rely on an unsupported step from facts to values, leave a key choice unexplained, fail to settle some conflicts, or settle them only by giving up part of the original promise of objectivity.

This result comes from testing the proposed systems. It is not a proof that every possible objective moral fact is impossible. It is also not the claim that nothing matters. Pain, relationships, choice, trust, evidence, institutions, power, and hope remain.

The final part stops trying to combine whole moral systems. It begins with emotions and values that people share or can at least understand. It then checks the facts, names who is affected, states the tradeoffs, and builds policies with safeguards, disagreement, review, and repair.
