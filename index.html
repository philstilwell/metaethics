Ten terms before you begin

Plain-language guide to the site’s recurring ideas

You do not need to memorize these. Open a term whenever a lab uses a phrase that feels unfamiliar.

Objective moral fact

A fact that would make something morally right, wrong, required, or deserved even if no person or group approved of that judgment.

Independent of approval

A claim meant to remain true even when the speaker, the audience, or an entire community rejects it. Philosophers sometimes call this “approval-independent.” It does not have to mean supernatural.

Moral non-realism

A broad family of views that reject at least some claims about objective moral facts. People in this family still disagree about truth, language, reasons, public rules, criticism, and which moral practices should remain.

Moral error theory

The view that ordinary moral claims try to report moral facts but fail because those facts do not exist.

Rules built through a process

Some views support rules through a stated way of reasoning, choosing, or reaching agreement. Philosophers often call these views “constructivist,” but they disagree about which processes work and whether the results count as moral truth.

Desert: suffering or praise for its own sake

The claim that someone should receive blame, pain, punishment, or praise simply because of what they knowingly did—not because the response protects, repairs, prevents, teaches, or changes anything.

Reason for action

A fact or consideration that supports doing something. It does not have to settle the choice. Several reasons can compete, and one can matter without being strongest.

Reason for belief

A fact or consideration that supports believing something. Evidence is a familiar example. Philosophers use the term “epistemic reason” for this kind of support.

Counting for one’s own sake

A being has “direct standing” when its own interests matter—not only its usefulness, owner, admirers, or connections. Counting directly does not guarantee equal weight or victory in every conflict.

Values with no shared unit

Some values cannot be fully converted into one common unit such as money, happiness, or lives saved. Philosophers call them “incommensurable.” People may still compare them by using priorities, protected limits, judgment, or a fair process.

How the tools interact

They take apart one familiar moral sentence.

“You ought not do that because it is wrong.”

A disagreement about this sentence may concern the action, the word “wrong,” the force of “ought,” the usefulness of speaking this way, the design of a shared rule, or several hidden disputes at once. The labs keep those questions apart.

  1. 01
    The Moral Profile tests the reason.

    Why oppose this action rather than another one?

  2. 02
    The Meaning Lab tests the claim.

    What would make “wrong” accurate, sincere, or useful?

  3. 03
    The Missing Ought tests the authority.

    Why should the demand govern someone who rejects it?

  4. 04
    After Moral Facts tests the policy.

    If no objective status exists, should the sentence be retired, preserved, or revised?

  5. 05
    The Disagreement Decoder tests the layer.

    Are opponents disputing evidence, words, interests, priorities, plans, rules, procedures, or alleged moral facts?

  6. 06
    The Value Genealogy Lab tests conclusions drawn from a belief’s history.

    Does the history only explain the belief, reveal an unreliable source, argue against the belief itself, or change who still owes support?

  7. 07
    The Community-Code Simulator tests construction.

    Which human reasons and procedures carry a rule when no independent moral fact settles it?

  8. 08
    The Social Criticism Lab tests reform.

    Can a dissenter use a group’s rules, the society’s own promises, stated human concerns, a fair process, or a claim that remains true despite rejection?

  9. 09
    The Blame Laboratory tests response.

    Does a consequence protect, deter, repair, rehabilitate, set terms, revise trust, protest publicly, or make deserved suffering its point?

  10. 10
    The Reasons Lab tests what “a reason” means.

    Does a fact only explain or motivate an action, or does it actually support that action? What could give it that support?

  11. 11
    The Evidence Lab tests reasons for belief.

    Why should evidence that points toward truth guide us? Does doubt about moral facts answer that separate question?

  12. 12
    The Standing Lab tests the boundary.

    Which beings or systems enter direct consideration, and what feature or rule puts them there?

  13. 13
    The Value Conflict Lab tests comparison.

    When important concerns pull apart, what ranks, constrains, weighs, or fairly settles them?

  14. 14
    The Moral Emotions Lab asks what remains.

    If nobody deserves pain or praise simply for a past act, which other roles can still support guilt, protest, gratitude, pride, or change?
