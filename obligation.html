The missing ought

When “wrong” only means “we disapprove.”

A community can condemn, shame, exclude, fine, or imprison. Those are real forces. But what turns any of them into a duty that still binds someone who rejects the community’s rule?

01

The central distinction

Pressure can explain obedience without establishing authority.

“They will punish me” explains why I might comply. It does not yet explain why their rule has authority over me when punishment, agreement, and approval are removed.

Before the test

“Our group enforces this rule” is not the same as “this rule applies even if you reject it.”

Social code

“These are our rules.”

A group can share values, demand conduct, and enforce consequences. That can be coherent and useful.

Duty claimed to apply even without approval

“The rule binds beyond approval.”

This stronger claim must explain why the duty remains when people reject the rule, escape punishment, or stand outside the group.

Exactly what claim does this lab test?

This lab calls a duty independent of approval when it is supposed to apply even if a person or whole community rejects it. Public support may help explain why a rule is followed, but support alone does not establish this stronger claim.

This is not a test of every view that rejects objective moral facts. A person may still appeal to harm, consistency, fair agreement, or careful reasoning. “Independent of approval” also does not automatically mean supernatural. The lab tests one narrow step: is the fact that a group disapproves enough, by itself, to create a duty that also applies outside that group?
